C1 — States of Matter

1. Solids, Liquids and Gases (C1.01)

Matter and the Three States

Matter is anything that occupies space and has mass. Chemistry is the study of how matter behaves, and of how one kind of substance can be changed into another.

Any substance can exist in three different states of matter — solid, liquid or gas — depending on the conditions of temperature and pressure. Changing the temperature and/or the pressure can change the state a substance is in: for example, gallium is a metal that melts with just the warmth of a hand.

The nature of matter:

  • Matter can be an element, a compound or a mixture.
  • Matter can be solid, liquid or gas; in every state it occupies a volume in space and has a mass.
  • Matter is made up of very small particles: atoms, molecules or ions.

Properties of the Three States

Property Solid Liquid Gas
Particle separation Packed close together Closely packed together Spread very far apart
Particle arrangement Regular arrangement, or lattice Irregular arrangement Totally irregular
Particle movement Not able to move freely; simply vibrate about fixed positions Able to move around, past each other Move rapidly and randomly
Volume Fixed volume Fixed volume No fixed volume; expands to fill the container
Shape Definite shape No definite shape; takes the shape of the container No definite shape; takes the shape of the container
Density High Moderate to high Low
Fluidity Does not flow Generally flows easily Flows easily
Effect of pressure on volume Unaffected Only slightly compressible Easily compressed

Key facts:

  • Liquids and gases are fluids: they can flow, so they can be poured or pumped from one container to another. A solid has a fixed shape and volume.
  • All three states expand (increase in volume) when heated and contract when cooled. The effect is much bigger for a gas than for a solid or a liquid.
  • At a fixed temperature, the volume of a gas is easily reduced by increasing the pressure: gases are easily compressed ('squashed').

Drawing particle diagrams (use circles of the same size for one substance):

  • Solid: circles touching each other in neat, regular rows.
  • Liquid: circles mostly touching, but in a jumbled, irregular arrangement with a few gaps.
  • Gas: only a few circles, spread far apart, with arrows to show random movement.

Exam tip: when asked to describe the particles in a state, give all three features: separation (close together or far apart), arrangement (regular or irregular) and movement (vibrating, moving past each other, or moving rapidly and randomly).

Changes of State

Large increases or decreases in temperature and pressure can cause a substance to change its physical state.

Change of state From → to Heat energy Key fact
Melting solid → liquid Absorbed A pure substance melts suddenly at one particular temperature, its melting point
Freezing (solidification) liquid → solid Released The reverse of melting; takes place sharply at the same temperature
Evaporation (vaporisation) liquid → gas Absorbed Takes place at the surface, over a range of temperatures below the boiling point
Boiling liquid → gas Absorbed Takes place throughout the liquid, at one specific temperature, the boiling point
Condensation (liquefaction) gas → liquid Released Usually caused by cooling; also possible by increasing the pressure
Sublimation solid → gas directly Absorbed (solid → gas) Only a few solids do this; the term is used for the change in either direction (not required knowledge)

Melting and Freezing

  • The melting point (m.p.) is the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. It always happens at one particular temperature for each substance.
  • When the liquid is cooled, the process is reversed at precisely the same temperature, called the freezing point (f.p.).
  • The melting point and the freezing point of a substance are the same temperature. For example, pure water melts and freezes at 0 °C.
Temperature of the substance Physical state
Below its melting point Solid
Between its melting point and its boiling point Liquid
Above its boiling point Gas

Worked Example: Deciding the Physical State

Iodine has a melting point of 114 °C and a boiling point of 184 °C. What state is iodine in at 25 °C, at 150 °C and at 200 °C?

  • Step 1: 25 °C is below the melting point (114 °C), so iodine is a solid.
  • Step 2: 150 °C is above the melting point but below the boiling point (184 °C), so iodine is a liquid.
  • Step 3: 200 °C is above the boiling point, so iodine is a gas.
  • Rule for room temperature (about 20–25 °C): a substance is a solid if its m.p. is above room temperature, a liquid if its m.p. is below room temperature and its b.p. is above it, and a gas if its b.p. is below room temperature.

Evaporation, Boiling and Condensation

Evaporation:

  • A liquid left with its surface exposed to the air evaporates: it changes into a vapour at a temperature below its boiling point.
  • Evaporation takes place only from the surface of the liquid.
  • The larger the surface area, the faster the liquid evaporates; the warmer the liquid, the faster it evaporates.

Boiling:

  • At a certain temperature, a liquid becomes hot enough for gas to form within the liquid and not just at the surface: bubbles of gas appear throughout the liquid.
  • Boiling takes place at a specific temperature for each pure liquid, its boiling point (b.p.).
Feature Evaporation Boiling
Where it takes place At the surface of the liquid only Throughout the liquid
Bubbles No bubbles Bubbles of gas form inside the liquid
Temperature Over a range of temperatures below the boiling point Only at the boiling point

Condensation:

  • Condensation is the reverse of evaporation: a vapour or gas changes into a liquid, and heat is given out to the surroundings.
  • It is usually brought about by cooling. The gas state is the one most affected by pressure, so at normal temperatures a gas can also be condensed by increasing the pressure, without cooling.

A boiling kettle: colourless, invisible water vapour escapes from the kettle; it is present in the clear region just at the spout. The visible cloud of steam is made of tiny droplets of liquid water, formed by condensation as the vapour cools in the air.

Volatility:

  • A volatile liquid evaporates easily. It has a low boiling point because there are only weak intermolecular forces between its molecules.
  • Volatility is the property of how easily a liquid evaporates.
  • Water (b.p. 100 °C) is quite a volatile liquid; ethanol (b.p. 78 °C) is even more volatile than water.
  • Rule: the lower the boiling point, the more volatile the liquid.

Boiling Point and Pressure

  • The boiling point of a liquid changes if the surrounding pressure changes. Boiling points are usually given at the pressure of the atmosphere at sea level (atmospheric pressure or standard pressure), where water boils at 100 °C.
  • If the surrounding pressure falls, the boiling point falls: on a high mountain, water boils below 100 °C.
  • If the surrounding pressure increases, the boiling point rises.
  • This is because a liquid boils when the pressure of the gas forming above it equals the atmospheric pressure.

Pure Substances

  • A pure substance consists of only one substance (a single element or compound) without any contaminating impurities. It melts and boils at definite, precise temperatures, so it has a sharp melting point.
  • Impurities change the melting and boiling points. An impure substance usually melts below and boils above the values for the pure substance, and it often melts or boils over a range of temperatures.
  • So melting and boiling points can be used to test the purity of a sample, and to check the identity of an unknown substance.
Property Pure substance Impure substance
Melting point Sharp, at one exact temperature Lower, and over a range of temperatures
Boiling point Sharp, at one exact temperature Higher, and over a range of temperatures
Cooling curve Clear flat section at the freezing point No sharp flat section

Effects of Temperature and Pressure on the Volume of a Gas

The space between the rapidly moving particles in a gas is much greater than in a solid or a liquid, so the volume of a gas is much more easily changed by temperature and pressure.

Change Effect on the volume of the gas Explanation using particles
Temperature increased Volume increases; the gas expands Particles move faster and more freely, so they occupy a greater volume
Temperature decreased Volume decreases; the gas contracts Particles move more slowly and occupy a smaller volume
External pressure increased Volume decreases; the gas is compressed Particles are pushed closer together
External pressure decreased Volume increases; the gas expands Particles occupy a greater space
  • Gases are easily compressed because the intermolecular space (the space between the particles) is large and can be reduced by increasing the external pressure. In liquids this space is very much smaller, so liquids are not very compressible.
  • Gas pressure is produced by the gas particles hitting the walls of the container. The more often the particles collide with the walls, the greater the pressure.

The Kinetic Particle Theory

The kinetic particle theory describes the three states of matter, and the changes between them, in terms of the movement of particles. Its main points are:

  • All matter is made up of very small particles; different substances contain different types of particle, such as atoms, molecules or ions.
  • The particles are moving all the time. The higher the temperature, the higher the average energy of the particles.
  • The freedom of movement and the arrangement of the particles are different in the three states.
  • The pressure of a gas is produced by the gas particles hitting the walls of the container.

Particles and structure:

  • An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction.
  • A molecule is a group of atoms held together by covalent bonds.
  • The ordered arrangement of particles in solids produces the regular crystal structures seen in solids. The ability of the particles to move in liquids and gases gives them their fluid properties.

Explaining Changes of State with Particles

Process What happens to the particles
Heating a solid The particles gain energy and vibrate more strongly; they occupy more space, so the solid expands
Melting Eventually the particles have enough energy to break the forces holding the lattice together, and they can move around: the solid melts
Heating a liquid The particles move faster and the liquid expands
Evaporation Some faster-moving particles at the surface have enough energy to escape into the gas; as the temperature rises, more particles have enough energy, so evaporation is faster
Boiling At the boiling point, the particles have enough energy to break the forces attracting them together; they move very fast and separate from each other
Condensation and freezing The reverse: the particles lose energy, slow down and come together; new forces of attraction form and energy is given out

Why evaporation cools a liquid: the particles that escape are the faster-moving ones, so the particles left behind have a lower average energy and the liquid cools down. The liquid takes in heat from anything it is touching, which is why a liquid evaporating from your skin feels cool.

Heating and Cooling Curves

Heating curve (heating a solid until it becomes a gas):

  • The temperature rises until the solid starts to melt.
  • The temperature stays constant during melting, until all the solid has melted.
  • The temperature then rises as the liquid warms up, until its boiling point is reached.
  • The temperature stays constant during boiling, until all the liquid has turned into gas.

Cooling curve (cooling a liquid until it becomes a solid):

  • The temperature falls as the liquid cools.
  • The level (horizontal) part of the curve is where the liquid freezes, forming the solid: the temperature stays constant while the liquid solidifies. For example, a liquid that freezes at −15 °C gives a flat section at −15 °C (a cooling mixture of ice and salt can be used to cool it below 0 °C).
  • When all of it has frozen, the temperature of the solid falls again.

Key idea: heat energy is needed to change a solid into a liquid, or a liquid into a gas. During the reverse processes, heat energy is given out.

Worked Example: Interpreting a Cooling Curve from Gas to Solid

A gas is cooled until it becomes a solid. Its cooling curve has three sloping sections (A, B and C) separated by two flat sections.

Section What is present Temperature Explanation
A (sloping) Gas Falling The energy of the particles decreases; they move more slowly and interact with each other more strongly
First flat section (condensation) Gas and liquid Constant The particles come together to form the liquid; energy is given out as the intermolecular forces form, so the temperature stays constant until all the gas has condensed
B (sloping) Liquid Falling The liquid cools; its particles slow down
Second flat section (freezing) Liquid and solid Constant The forces holding the solid together form and energy is given out, keeping the temperature constant until freezing is complete
C (sloping) Solid Falling The particles in the solid vibrate less strongly as the temperature falls

Why the temperature stays constant during a change of state:

  • On cooling: as the particles come closer together, new forces of attraction form, so energy is given out. This keeps the temperature unchanged until the liquid or solid is totally formed.
  • On heating: the energy put in is used to overcome the forces between the particles, instead of raising the temperature, until the change of state is complete.

Endothermic and Exothermic Changes of State

Type of change Changes of state Heat energy ΔH
Endothermic Melting, evaporation, boiling Taken in from the surroundings, to overcome the forces between the particles Positive
Exothermic Condensation, freezing Given out to the surroundings, as forces form between the particles Negative
  • On a heating curve, the flat sections are at the melting point (solid and liquid both present) and the boiling point (liquid and gas both present).
  • On a cooling curve, the flat sections are where the gas condenses (gas and liquid both present) and where the liquid freezes (liquid and solid both present).

Practical: Plotting a Cooling Curve

  • Two solids are melted in boiling tubes in a water-bath at 90 °C: A is paraffin wax (m.p. around 55 °C) and B is octadecanoic acid (stearic acid, m.p. 70 °C) or phenyl salicylate (salol, m.p. 43 °C).
  • When each has melted, a thermometer is placed in it (with the bulb covered by liquid), the tubes are stood in an empty beaker, and the temperature in each is recorded every minute until it reaches 40 °C.
  • A graph is plotted for each substance with time on the x-axis and temperature on the y-axis.
  • Result: the pure substance (B) gives a clear flat section at its freezing point. Paraffin wax is a mixture, so it solidifies over a range of temperatures and has no sharp flat section.
  • Safety: wear eye protection throughout, and follow your teacher's instructions about the Bunsen burner flame.

2. Diffusion (C1.02)

What Is Diffusion?

Diffusion is the process by which different fluids mix as a result of the random motion of their particles. Observations of diffusion, where particles spread to fill the space available to them, gave some of the earliest evidence for the kinetic particle model.

Main ideas:

  • Particles move from a region of higher concentration towards a region of lower concentration. Eventually the particles are evenly spread, and their concentration is the same throughout.
  • The rate of diffusion in liquids is much slower than in gases.
  • Diffusion does not take place in solids, because the particles cannot move from place to place.

Diffusion in Liquids

  • Potassium manganate(VII) in water: a crystal is placed at the bottom of a dish of water and left to stand. At first the water around the crystal turns purple as the solid dissolves and particles move off its surface into the water. After some time the crystal dissolves completely, and the purple colour spreads until the particles are evenly spread through the whole solution.
  • Silver nitrate and potassium iodide: a crystal of each is placed at opposite sides of a Petri dish of deionised water. As the crystals dissolve, their ions diffuse through the water, and where they meet an insoluble yellow precipitate of silver iodide forms.
  • Safety for this practical: wear eye protection, use tweezers to handle the crystals, and wash your hands afterwards. Silver nitrate is corrosive and oxidising, can stain the skin and is hazardous to the aquatic environment, so waste silver nitrate solution must not be poured down the drain.
  • Dissolved gases diffuse too, which is especially important in breathing: diffusion moves oxygen from the lungs into the blood, and carbon dioxide from the blood into the lungs.

Diffusion of Gases

  • A few drops of liquid bromine are put into a gas jar and the lid is replaced. Liquid bromine is highly volatile, so it evaporates easily: Br₂(l) → Br₂(g). The brown gas spreads throughout the jar until it completely fills the container. Gases diffuse to fill all the space available to them.
  • Diffusion is the way that smells reach us.
  • Gas particles move at high speeds: the nitrogen and oxygen molecules in the air travel at about 1800 km/h. However, they collide with other particles many millions of times per second, so their path is not direct. These frequent collisions slow down the overall rate of diffusion from one place to another.

Mass and Diffusion

Not all particles diffuse at the same rate: the speed of diffusion depends on the mass of the particles. At the same temperature, molecules with a lower mass move, on average, faster than those with a higher mass.

  • Heavier gas particles move more slowly than lighter particles at the same temperature.
  • Larger molecules therefore diffuse more slowly than smaller ones.
  • The rate of diffusion is inversely related to the mass of the particles: the lower the relative molecular mass (Mr), the faster the gas diffuses.
  • The average speed of the particles increases with temperature, so diffusion is faster at higher temperatures.

The ammonia and hydrochloric acid experiment:

  • Cotton wool soaked in concentrated ammonia solution is placed at one end of a long glass tube, and cotton wool soaked in concentrated hydrochloric acid at the other end. Ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas are given off and diffuse along the tube.
  • Where the two gases meet, they react to form a white smoke ring of fine particles of solid ammonium chloride: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s).
  • The ring is not formed halfway along the tube; it forms nearer the hydrochloric acid end. This shows that ammonia, the lighter molecule, diffuses faster.

Worked Example: Explaining Where the Smoke Ring Forms

  • Step 1 — work out Mr: NH₃ = 14 + (3 × 1) = 17; HCl = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5.
  • Step 2 — compare masses: ammonia has the lower Mr, so its molecules move faster, on average, at the same temperature.
  • Step 3 — compare distances: in the same time, ammonia molecules travel further along the tube than hydrogen chloride molecules.
  • Step 4 — conclusion: the gases meet, and the white ring of ammonium chloride forms, closer to the hydrochloric acid end of the tube.

The Porous Pot Experiment

  • A porous pot is an unglazed pot with channels (pores) through which gases can pass. In this experiment, a porous pot cylinder is fitted on top of one side of a U-tube containing coloured liquid.
  • With air inside and outside the pot, the liquid levels are the same on both sides.
  • When a large beaker of hydrogen is placed over the pot (hydrogen outside, air inside), hydrogen molecules (Mr = 2) diffuse into the pot faster than the heavier nitrogen (Mr = 28) and oxygen (Mr = 32) molecules of the air diffuse out. The pressure inside the pot increases, so the liquid is pushed down on the pot side and rises on the other side.

Factors Affecting the Rate of Diffusion

Factor Effect on the rate of diffusion Reason
State of matter Fast in gases, much slower in liquids, none in solids Gas particles move fast and freely; solid particles only vibrate about fixed positions
Mass of the particles Lower Mr gives faster diffusion Lighter particles move faster at the same temperature
Temperature Higher temperature gives faster diffusion The particles have more energy and move faster
Collisions with other particles Slow diffusion down The particles' paths are not direct

4. Key Equations

Changes of State

  1. solid → liquid: melting, at the melting point (heat absorbed)
  2. liquid → solid: freezing (solidification), at the freezing point, which equals the melting point (heat released)
  3. liquid → gas: evaporation (at the surface, below the boiling point) or boiling (throughout the liquid, at the boiling point) (heat absorbed)
  4. gas → liquid: condensation (liquefaction) (heat released)
  5. solid → gas directly: sublimation

Changes of State with State Symbols

  1. H₂O(s) → H₂O(l) — ice melts at 0 °C
  2. H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) — water boils at 100 °C at atmospheric pressure
  3. Br₂(l) → Br₂(g) — liquid bromine evaporates

Reactions Used to Show Diffusion

  1. ammonia + hydrogen chloride → ammonium chloride
  2. NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s) — white smoke
  3. silver nitrate + potassium iodide → silver iodide + potassium nitrate
  4. AgNO₃(aq) + KI(aq) → AgI(s) + KNO₃(aq) — yellow precipitate

Predicting Physical State and Volatility

  1. Temperature below the m.p. → solid
  2. Temperature between the m.p. and the b.p. → liquid
  3. Temperature above the b.p. → gas
  4. Lower boiling point → more volatile liquid

Gas Volume, Temperature and Pressure

  1. Temperature increased → volume increases (the gas expands)
  2. Temperature decreased → volume decreases (the gas contracts)
  3. Pressure increased → volume decreases (the gas is compressed)
  4. Pressure decreased → volume increases (the gas expands)
  5. Surrounding pressure decreased → boiling point decreases; surrounding pressure increased → boiling point increases

Relative Molecular Mass and Diffusion

  1. Mr = sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms in the molecule
  2. Mr(NH₃) = 14 + (3 × 1) = 17
  3. Mr(HCl) = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5
  4. Mr(CH₃NH₂, methylamine) = 12 + (5 × 1) + 14 = 31
  5. Mr(H₂) = 2 × 1 = 2; Mr(N₂) = 2 × 14 = 28; Mr(O₂) = 2 × 16 = 32; Mr(Br₂) = 2 × 80 = 160
  6. Lower Mr → faster diffusion (at the same temperature); higher temperature → faster diffusion

Energy Changes

  1. Melting, evaporation and boiling: endothermic (ΔH positive)
  2. Condensation and freezing: exothermic (ΔH negative)

5. Practice Questions with Full Answers

Before You Start — Question 1

Think about water heating in a pan. Bubbles form in the water and water vapour is formed above the pan. If the same pan of water is then placed in a freezer, solid ice will form. What is happening to the water molecules? How are they behaving differently in each of these forms?

Answer:

  • Heating: the water molecules gain energy and move faster. Some escape from the surface (evaporation). At the boiling point (100 °C), molecules throughout the liquid have enough energy to overcome the forces attracting them to each other, so bubbles of water vapour form inside the liquid: the water boils.
  • Above the pan: the molecules of water vapour are very far apart and move rapidly and randomly in all directions.
  • In the freezer: the molecules lose energy and slow down. At 0 °C they become held in fixed positions in a regular arrangement (a lattice), so the water freezes into ice. In ice the molecules can only vibrate.
Form of water Arrangement of molecules Movement of molecules
Ice (solid) Close together in a regular lattice Vibrate about fixed positions
Liquid water Close together in an irregular arrangement Move around, sliding past each other
Water vapour (gas) Very far apart, totally irregular Move rapidly and randomly

Before You Start — Question 2

Spray a small amount of air freshener at the front of a room. How does the smell spread around the room? Shake the can. Can you hear the liquid in it? Or try placing some liquid perfume in a dish in front of you and see how long it takes for someone to notice the scent some distance away. Discuss these observations in terms of changes of state and the movement of the molecules involved.

Answer:

  • When you shake the can you can hear liquid moving: the air freshener is stored as a liquid, kept under pressure inside the can.
  • When it is sprayed, it comes out as tiny droplets that quickly evaporate, changing into a gas (vapour).
  • The scent molecules then spread around the room by diffusion. They move randomly, colliding with air molecules, and spread from where they are concentrated (the front of the room) to where there are fewer of them, until they are evenly spread through the room.
  • Perfume in a dish evaporates from its surface, and the perfume molecules then diffuse through the air. It takes some time for someone at a distance to notice the scent, because each perfume molecule collides with air molecules millions of times per second, so its path is not direct and the overall rate of diffusion is slowed down.

Before You Start — Question 3

Look at the flowchart of the nature of matter (Figure C1.01). It shows that matter: can be elements, compounds or mixtures; can be solid, liquid or gas (and in each state occupies a volume in space and has a mass); and is made up of particles, which are atoms, molecules or ions. Working in groups, can you add more detail to improve and extend the flowchart?

Answer: Extra boxes that could be added to the branches:

  • Elements contain only one type of atom (for example iron or oxygen). Compounds contain two or more elements chemically combined (for example water, H₂O). Mixtures contain two or more substances that are not chemically combined (for example air or sea water).
  • Solid: fixed shape and volume; particles packed close together in a regular lattice, vibrating about fixed positions.
  • Liquid: fixed volume but takes the shape of its container; particles close together in an irregular arrangement, moving past each other.
  • Gas: no fixed shape or volume, so it spreads to fill its container and is easily compressed; particles far apart, moving rapidly and randomly.
  • Changes of state link the three states: melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation, caused by changes in temperature and/or pressure.
  • Particles: an atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction; a molecule is a group of atoms held together by covalent bonds; an ion is an electrically charged particle.
  • Kinetic particle theory: the particles are always moving, and they move faster at higher temperatures; their random movement causes diffusion in liquids and gases.
  • Mass and volume are linked by density: solids have a high density, liquids a moderate to high density and gases a low density.

Science in Context C1.01 — Discussion Question 1

Saturn's rings are streams of icy particles made of water (H₂O), ranging in size from tiny snowflakes to 'snowballs' as large as a house. Saturn's atmosphere contains clouds of ammonia and methane, but the bulk of the planet is made of hydrogen and helium gases. Deep in the centre is a small rocky core surrounded by a liquid layer of these gases; the hydrogen is liquid because of the high pressure near the core, and this liquid hydrogen shows metallic properties, which produce the planet's magnetic field.

Why are the planets Jupiter and Saturn called 'gas giants'? What progression do we see in the physical nature of the planets as we move away from the Sun?

Answer:

  • Jupiter and Saturn are called gas giants because they are enormous planets made mostly of the gases hydrogen and helium. They have no solid surface, only a small rocky core deep inside.
  • Moving away from the Sun, the planets change from small rocky planets with solid surfaces (Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars), to the huge gas giants (Jupiter and Saturn), and then to Uranus and Neptune, which are giant planets made of gases and ices (often called 'ice giants').
  • The further a planet is from the Sun, the colder it is. So in the outer solar system, substances such as water, ammonia and methane are found frozen as ices, like the water ice in Saturn's rings.

Science in Context C1.01 — Discussion Question 2

Why does hydrogen only exist as a liquid under such extreme conditions of temperature and pressure?

Answer:

  • The forces of attraction between hydrogen molecules are extremely weak, so hydrogen has a very low boiling point (about −253 °C). At ordinary temperatures its molecules have far too much energy to stay close together, so hydrogen is a gas.
  • To make hydrogen a liquid, its molecules must be brought close together. This needs either an extremely low temperature, so that the molecules move slowly enough for the weak forces to hold them together, or an enormous pressure, which forces the molecules close together.
  • Deep inside Saturn the pressure is so great that the hydrogen is squeezed into a liquid.

Question C1.01

State the names for the following physical changes:

(a) liquid to solid

(b) liquid to gas at a precise temperature

(c) gas to liquid.

Answer:

(a) Freezing (also called solidification).

(b) Boiling. It takes place at a precise temperature, the boiling point; evaporation is not correct here, because it takes place over a range of temperatures.

(c) Condensation (also called liquefaction).

The melting and boiling points of three pure substances are given in the table.

(a) All three substances have negative values for their melting point. Which of them has the lowest melting point?

(a) Methane (m.p. −182 °C), the most negative value.

(b) Ethanol and mercury. Room temperature is about 20–25 °C. Both have a melting point below room temperature (−117 °C and −30 °C), so they have melted, and a boiling point above room temperature (78 °C and 357 °C), so they have not boiled. Methane is not a liquid: its boiling point (−164 °C) is far below room temperature, so it is a gas.

(c) An impurity lowers the freezing point of the liquid, and the liquid freezes over a range of temperatures instead of sharply at one temperature. For example, salt lowers the freezing point of water, which is why a mixture of ice and salt can cool things below 0 °C.

Question C1.04

Iodine is often seen as an example of a substance that changes directly from a solid to a gas. However, data books that give the standard physical measurements for substances show values for the melting point (114 °C) and boiling point (184 °C) of iodine at atmospheric pressure.

(a) Explain why iodine seems to miss out the liquid stage if crystals are heated strongly in a boiling tube.

(b) Suggest how you could demonstrate that iodine can melt to form a liquid at atmospheric pressure.

Answer:

(a) Solid iodine is volatile: even below its melting point, particles escape from the surface of the crystals as a purple vapour. When the crystals are heated strongly, the temperature rises quickly through the short liquid range (only 70 °C between the melting point, 114 °C, and the boiling point, 184 °C), so any liquid that forms turns into vapour almost at once and is not noticed. The vapour then turns straight back into solid crystals on the cooler upper part of the boiling tube. So iodine appears to change directly from a solid into a gas (and back again), missing out the liquid stage.

(b) Heat the iodine gently and steadily so that its temperature rises slowly to just above 114 °C but stays well below 184 °C. For example, stand a boiling tube containing a fairly large amount of iodine crystals in an oil bath at about 120–130 °C, with a thermometer to check the temperature (a water-bath cannot be used, because water boils at 100 °C), and loosely plug the tube so that the vapour stays around the crystals. The crystals will be seen to melt into a dark liquid at about 114 °C. Alternatively, put a few crystals in a melting-point tube (a narrow tube sealed at one end) and heat it slowly in a melting-point apparatus. Work in a fume cupboard and wear eye protection, because iodine vapour is harmful.

In this experiment, cooling curves are plotted for two substances. Substance A is paraffin wax (m.p. around 55 °C). Experimental Skills C1.01 — Question 2

Explain any ways in which your method could be improved to give more reliable results.

Answer:

  • Stir each liquid gently with the stirring thermometer as it cools, so that the temperature is the same throughout and the reading is representative.
  • Take readings more often (for example every 30 seconds), especially near the freezing point, so that the flat section is shown clearly.
  • Repeat the experiment and check that the results agree, for example that the flat section occurs at the same temperature each time; use mean values.
  • Use a more precise thermometer, or a digital temperature probe with a data logger, which records continuously and avoids reading errors.
  • Use the same amount of each substance, make sure the thermometer bulb is fully covered and not touching the glass, and let both tubes cool in the same conditions, away from draughts, so that they cool at a steady rate.

What factors control where the solid is formed in the Petri dish?

Why does the solid not form exactly in the middle of the dish?

Question C1.07

A small amount of liquid bromine is placed in a gas jar, which is then sealed with a lid. Evaporation of the liquid bromine takes place:

Br₂(l) → Br₂(g)

Use the ideas of the kinetic theory to explain why, after about an hour, the gaseous bromine molecules have spread to evenly occupy the whole container.

Answer:

  • Bromine is volatile, so it evaporates and molecules of bromine gas form just above the liquid, where their concentration is high.
  • According to the kinetic theory, gas molecules are in constant, rapid and random motion. The bromine molecules collide with air molecules and with each other, changing direction each time, so they move in all directions.
  • As a result there is an overall movement of bromine molecules from where their concentration is high (near the liquid) to where it is lower (higher up the jar): this is diffusion. At the same time, air molecules diffuse in the opposite direction.
  • This continues until the bromine molecules are evenly spread and their concentration (and the brown colour) is the same throughout the whole jar.
  • It takes about an hour, not a moment, because each bromine molecule collides with air molecules many millions of times per second, so its path is not direct. Bromine molecules are also heavy (Mr = 160), so they move relatively slowly.

Question C1.08

A teacher carried out a class demonstration on diffusion similar to the one using ammonia (Mr = 17) and hydrochloric acid (Mr = 36.5), in which a white smoke ring of ammonium chloride forms inside a long tube where the two gases meet. However, they replaced the ammonia with methylamine (Mr = 31), which reacts in a similar way to ammonia (Mr is the relative molecular mass of the substance).

(a) Where would you predict the position of the smoke ring to be in this experiment? Explain your answer.

(b) Suggest other gases similar to hydrochloric acid that could replace it in this demonstration (use textbooks or the internet to find a possible acid).

Answer:

(a) The smoke ring would form close to the middle of the tube, but still slightly nearer the hydrochloric acid end. Methylamine (Mr = 31) is lighter than hydrogen chloride (Mr = 36.5), so its molecules move slightly faster and travel slightly further in the same time. However, the difference in mass is much smaller than between ammonia (17) and hydrogen chloride (36.5), so the two gases diffuse at more similar rates, and the ring forms much nearer the middle than in the ammonia experiment.

Question C1.09

Experiments comparing the rate of diffusion of different gases can be done using a porous pot. A cylinder made of porous pot is fitted on top of one side of a U-tube containing coloured liquid; the other side of the U-tube is open. Gas molecules are able to pass through the porous pot. Any change in pressure inside the pot will cause a change in the liquid levels in the U-tube. When there is air both inside and outside the pot, the liquid levels are the same. When a large beaker of hydrogen is placed over the pot (hydrogen outside, air inside), the liquid level becomes higher on the open side.

Explain why the levels of liquid change when hydrogen is placed outside the porous pot cylinder.

Answer: Hydrogen molecules (Mr = 2) are much lighter than the nitrogen (Mr = 28) and oxygen (Mr = 32) molecules in air, so at the same temperature they move much faster. Gas molecules can pass through the porous pot in both directions, but hydrogen diffuses into the pot faster than air diffuses out. Activity C1.01 — Planning the Model

Working in a small group, create a model or visual representation that explains the movement of the particles in the different states. How will you arrange the particles to demonstrate solids, liquids and gases? How could you represent the movement of the particles?

Answer: One possible model uses marbles in a tray:

  • Particles: identical marbles (or students, in a people model).
  • Solid: the tray is filled with marbles packed tightly in neat rows. Shaking the tray gently makes the marbles jiggle (vibrate) but they stay in place.

Activity C1.01 — Question 1

Why can three states of matter exist?

Answer: The particles of a substance attract each other, and they also have kinetic energy because they are always moving. The state depends on the balance between these two things. When the particles have little energy, the forces of attraction hold them in fixed positions in a lattice (solid). With more energy, they can move past each other but are still held close together (liquid). With enough energy to overcome the forces completely, they separate and move freely (gas). Changing the temperature or pressure changes this balance, so the substance changes state.

Activity C1.01 — Question 2

Why does it take time for a solid to melt?

Answer: Melting needs energy to overcome the forces holding the particles in the lattice. This heat energy has to be transferred into the solid from its surroundings, which takes time, so the outside melts first and heat must then reach the inside. While the solid is melting, the temperature stays at the melting point: all the energy taken in is used to break the forces between the particles, until the whole lattice has broken down.

Activity C1.01 — Question 3

Why do solids not diffuse over a normal time period?

Answer: The particles in a solid are held in fixed positions in a lattice by forces of attraction. They can only vibrate about these positions and cannot move from place to place, so they cannot spread out and mix with other particles.

Activity C1.01 — Question 4

What is different about substances that means that they each have different melting points?

Answer: Different substances are made of different particles (atoms, molecules or ions), and the strength of the forces of attraction between their particles is different. The stronger the forces holding the particles together in the solid, the more energy is needed to overcome them, and the higher the melting point.

Activity C1.01 — Question 5

Different substances also have different boiling points. Is the reason for this similar to why they have different melting points?

Answer: Yes. Boiling also needs energy to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles, but this time the particles must be separated completely. Substances with stronger forces between their particles have higher boiling points, while substances with only weak intermolecular forces, such as ethanol, have low boiling points and are volatile. Because the particles must be pulled completely apart, a substance's boiling point is always higher than its melting point.

Activity C1.01 — Question 6

Why is it that you can feel a liquid cool when it evaporates in your hand?

Answer: During evaporation, the fastest-moving particles (those with the most energy) escape from the surface of the liquid. The particles left behind have a lower average energy, so the temperature of the liquid falls. Evaporation is also endothermic: the liquid takes in heat energy from your hand as it evaporates, so your skin feels cool.

Project C1.01 — Question 2

The 'Goldilocks Zone': Earth orbits the Sun at just the right distance for liquid water to exist on its surface. It is neither too hot nor too cold for this. Research this situation, which is known as the 'Goldilocks Zone', and its meaning. Then think about how it applies to the orbits of Venus, Earth and Mars.

Answer:

  • Meaning: the Goldilocks Zone (also called the habitable zone) is the range of distances from a star where a planet's surface is neither too hot nor too cold for water to exist as a liquid. It is named after the fairy tale in which Goldilocks chooses the porridge that is 'just right'.
  • Venus is closer to the Sun than Earth and is too hot. Its thick atmosphere of carbon dioxide traps heat (a very strong greenhouse effect), so its surface is about 460 °C, far above the boiling point of water, and there is no liquid water.
  • Earth is inside the zone. Its average surface temperature is about 15 °C, so water exists as a solid, a liquid and a gas.

(c) Evaporation.

(d) Freezing is the change of state from a liquid to a solid, which takes place at the freezing point (the same temperature as the melting point).

Practice Question 5

A teacher opens a bottle of perfume at the front of her laboratory. She notices a smell of flowers. A few minutes later, students at the front of the lab notice the smell too. Those students at the back do not notice it until later.

(a) Give the two processes that must take place for the smell from the perfume to reach the back of the laboratory. [2]

Later in the day, when the room had cooled, the teacher tries the same experiment with a different class. The smell is the same but it takes longer to reach the back of the lab.

(b) Explain this observation by reference to the particles of perfume. [2]

[Total: 4]

Answer:

(a) Evaporation: the liquid perfume changes into a vapour (gas) (1 mark). Diffusion: the perfume particles spread through the air, by random movement, from where they are concentrated (near the bottle) to where they are less concentrated (the back of the lab) (1 mark).

(b) In the cooler room the perfume particles have less kinetic energy, so they move more slowly (1 mark). The perfume therefore evaporates more slowly and its particles diffuse through the air more slowly, so they take longer to reach the back of the lab (1 mark).

Practice Question 6

The figure shows the change in temperature as a substance cools down. On the graph of temperature (°C) against time (s), the temperature falls from point A to point B; B is at the melting point, marked by a dashed line. From B to C the line is horizontal, and from C to D the temperature falls again.

(a) Describe what is happening to the substance between C and D. [2]

(b) Describe what is happening to the particles of the substance between A and B. [2]

(c) State why the temperature does not change between B and C. [1]

[Total: 5]

Answer:

(a) Between C and D the substance has completely frozen, so it is all solid (1 mark), and the solid is cooling down: its temperature is falling as its particles vibrate less and less strongly (1 mark).

(b) Between A and B the substance is a liquid that is cooling. Its particles lose energy and move more slowly (1 mark); they are still close together and moving past each other, but they move closer together and attract each other more strongly as the temperature falls towards the freezing point (1 mark).

(c) The substance is freezing (changing from liquid to solid). As the particles form the solid, forces of attraction form between them and energy is given out; this balances the heat lost to the surroundings, so the temperature stays constant until all of the liquid has turned into a solid.

Practice Question 7

Ammonia gas (Mr = 17) is a base that changes universal indicator to purple. Hydrogen chloride gas (Mr = 36.5) is an acid that changes universal indicator to red. The figure shows an experiment done with these two gases: a long glass tube has cotton wool soaked in ammonia at the left-hand end, cotton wool soaked in hydrochloric acid at the right-hand end, and a piece of universal indicator paper in the middle. After two minutes, the universal indicator paper changed to purple.

(a) Explain why the universal indicator changed to purple and not red. [3]

(b) A further experiment measures the rates of diffusion of ammonia and hydrogen chloride. Two identical tubes are used: one has cotton wool soaked in ammonia at one end and the other has cotton wool soaked in hydrochloric acid at one end, and each has universal indicator paper the same distance away, near the other end. In the ammonia tube the colour changed in 34 s. Give an estimate for how long it took for the indicator paper in the HCl tube to change colour, and justify your answer. [2]

[Total: 5]

Answer:

(a) Ammonia has a lower relative molecular mass (Mr = 17) than hydrogen chloride (Mr = 36.5) (1 mark). So ammonia molecules move faster and diffuse faster through the air in the tube (1 mark). Both gases have the same distance to travel to the paper in the middle, so ammonia reaches the paper first, and the paper turns purple because ammonia is a base (1 mark).

(b) About 50 s (any sensible time clearly longer than 34 s) (1 mark). Hydrogen chloride molecules are heavier than ammonia molecules (Mr 36.5 compared with 17), so they move more slowly and take longer to diffuse the same distance along the tube (1 mark).

Working for the estimate (a rule beyond the syllabus: the time taken is proportional to the square root of Mr): time = 34 s × √(36.5 ÷ 17) = 34 s × 1.47 = 50 s (2 significant figures).

6. Exam Focus Priorities (Ranked by Frequency)

  1. Particles in solids, liquids and gases — describing separation, arrangement and movement; drawing or identifying particle diagrams; linking them to properties such as shape, volume, flow, density and compressibility.
  2. Naming changes of state — melting, freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation; identifying the change from a particle diagram or a description.
  3. Heating and cooling curves — identifying the state or states present in each section; explaining why the temperature stays constant during melting, boiling, condensing and freezing.
  4. Diffusion and the kinetic particle theory — random movement from high to low concentration; examples such as smells, bromine and potassium manganate(VII); why diffusion is slower in liquids and does not happen in solids.
  5. Relative molecular mass and the rate of diffusion — the ammonia and hydrogen chloride ring, predicting positions or times from Mr values, and the porous pot with hydrogen.
  6. Using melting and boiling point data — deducing the state at room temperature or at a given temperature; ordering liquids by volatility; a pure substance has a sharp m.p., while impurities lower the m.p. and widen the range.
  7. Effect of temperature on evaporation and diffusion — warmer particles have more energy and move faster, so evaporation and diffusion are faster (for example, perfume spreading in a cooler room).
  8. Evaporation versus boiling — surface versus throughout; below the b.p. versus at the b.p.; why evaporation cools; the effect of pressure on the boiling point.
  9. Effects of temperature and pressure on gas volume — expansion, contraction and compression explained by particle spacing and collisions; why gases are compressible but liquids and solids are not.
  10. Energy changes during changes of state — melting, evaporation and boiling are endothermic; condensation and freezing are exothermic.

7. Quick Revision Card

  • Matter occupies space and has mass; it exists as a solid, liquid or gas, depending on temperature and pressure.
  • Solid: particles packed close together in a regular lattice, vibrating about fixed positions; fixed shape and volume; does not flow.
  • Liquid: particles close together in an irregular arrangement, moving past each other; fixed volume, takes the shape of its container, flows.
  • Gas: particles very far apart, moving rapidly and randomly; no fixed shape or volume, fills its container, easily compressed.
  • Liquids and gases are fluids (they can flow); all states expand on heating, gases the most.
  • Changes of state: melting (solid → liquid), freezing (liquid → solid), evaporation and boiling (liquid → gas), condensation (gas → liquid).
  • m.p. = f.p. for any substance (water: 0 °C). A pure substance has a sharp m.p. and b.p.; impurities lower the m.p., raise the b.p. and spread them over a range.
  • Evaporation: at the surface only, below the b.p., faster when warmer or with a larger surface area. Boiling: bubbles throughout the liquid, only at the b.p.
  • Volatile means evaporates easily and has a low b.p. (ethanol, 78 °C, is more volatile than water, 100 °C).
  • Boiling point falls when the surrounding pressure falls (water boils below 100 °C on a high mountain) and rises when the pressure rises.
  • Gas volume: heating makes a gas expand, cooling makes it contract, and increasing the pressure compresses it. Gas pressure is caused by particles hitting the container walls.
  • Heating and cooling curves: the temperature stays constant during a change of state while energy is absorbed (melting, boiling) or released (condensing, freezing).
  • Endothermic: melting, evaporation, boiling (ΔH positive). Exothermic: condensation, freezing (ΔH negative).
  • Diffusion: the random movement of particles from high to low concentration until they are evenly spread; fast in gases, slow in liquids, none in solids.
  • Lighter particles diffuse faster: NH₃ (Mr 17) diffuses faster than HCl (Mr 36.5), so the white NH₄Cl ring forms nearer the HCl end. Higher temperature also means faster diffusion.
